\begin{abstract}
Audio recordings are frequently degraded by lossy compression codecs such as
MP3 and OGG Vorbis, resulting in audible artifacts and loss of high-frequency
content. We present \textbf{AudioRestore}, a proof-of-concept system for
text-controlled audio quality restoration built by fine-tuning SonicMaster, a
0.9-billion-parameter generative model that uses flow matching conditioned on
natural language prompts. We construct a codec-degraded dataset of 1{,}000
paired clips by applying MP3 and OGG compression at various bitrates
(24--128\,kbps) to clean music from MUSDB18-HQ. Fine-tuning on an
NVIDIA A100 GPU for 30 epochs reduces the flow-matching loss from 0.33 to
0.10. Quantitative evaluation reveals that the generative model produces
consistent output quality ($\sim$8\,dB SDR) regardless of input degradation
severity, trading sample-level fidelity for perceptual plausibility---a known
property of flow-matching models. Spectrogram analysis confirms the model
synthesizes high-frequency content absent in the degraded input, and prompt
variation experiments show the text-controlled interface remains functional
after fine-tuning. Our work demonstrates both the promise and limitations of
adapting generative audio models for codec artifact removal.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
audio restoration, codec artifacts, flow matching, SonicMaster, fine-tuning,
text-controlled audio enhancement
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Billions of audio files exist in compressed formats. Streaming services like
Spotify Free deliver music at 128\,kbps, YouTube rips circulate at even lower
bitrates, and decades of MP3 collections were encoded with aggressive
compression. Lossy codecs introduce audible artifacts---pre-echo, spectral
holes, and loss of high-frequency detail---that degrade the listening
experience~\cite{li2025apollo}.

Traditional audio restoration relies on manual signal processing chains:
equalization, de-noising, and spectral repair, each requiring expert tuning.
The rise of generative models---from denoising diffusion~\cite{ho2020denoising}
to latent diffusion for audio~\cite{liu2023audioldm} and diffusion-based
audio inverse solvers~\cite{moliner2023diffusion}---has opened new avenues for
learned restoration. Recent models such as Apollo~\cite{li2025apollo} and
SonicMaster~\cite{melechovsky2025sonicmaster} automate this process, but
general-purpose models may not optimally handle specific degradation types.

We propose \textbf{AudioRestore}, a system that fine-tunes SonicMaster on
codec-specific degradations. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{codec-degraded dataset} of 1{,}000 paired 30-second clips
    at various MP3 and OGG bitrates, annotated with natural language prompts.
    \item A \textbf{fine-tuning procedure} that adapts 522M trainable
    parameters of SonicMaster's flow-matching model to specialize in codec
    artifact removal while preserving its text-controlled interface.
    \item A \textbf{quantitative and qualitative evaluation} revealing that
    generative restoration models trade sample-level fidelity (SDR) for
    perceptual plausibility, with analysis of text prompt robustness.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Audio Super-Resolution and Bandwidth Extension}
Audio super-resolution aims to reconstruct high-frequency content from
bandwidth-limited signals. Methods range from traditional interpolation to
deep learning approaches using CNNs and GANs~\cite{su2021bandwidth}, including
neural vocoders like HiFi-GAN~\cite{kong2020hifigan} that can synthesize
high-fidelity waveforms from compressed representations. More recently,
diffusion-based methods have been applied to audio inverse
problems~\cite{moliner2023diffusion}, and latent diffusion models have shown
strong text-to-audio generation capabilities~\cite{liu2023audioldm}. These
methods typically operate in a fixed input-output configuration without
user control.

\subsection{Apollo}
Apollo~\cite{li2025apollo} is a band-sequence model for music restoration in
compressed audio, published at ICASSP 2025. It splits the spectrogram into 80
frequency bands, processes them through Roformer attention layers with
inter-band communication blocks (ICB), and reconstructs the waveform via a GAN
framework. Apollo achieves strong results on MUSDB18-HQ but operates without
any text conditioning---it is a fixed input-to-output mapping.

\subsection{Generative Models for Audio}
Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs)~\cite{ho2020denoising}
have achieved remarkable results in image and audio generation. AudioLDM~\cite{liu2023audioldm} applies latent diffusion to text-to-audio
generation, operating in a compressed VAE latent space for efficiency.
Flow matching~\cite{lipman2023flow} offers a simpler alternative to
diffusion, learning a deterministic velocity field that transports samples
from noise to data. TangoFlux~\cite{ghosh2024tangoflux} applies flow matching
to audio generation with CLAP-ranked preference optimization. These
generative approaches produce high-quality audio but, as we demonstrate, may
not improve reference-based metrics when applied to restoration.

\subsection{SonicMaster}
SonicMaster~\cite{melechovsky2025sonicmaster} is a unified generative model
for music restoration and mastering, submitted to ICLR 2026. It addresses five
categories of audio artifacts (equalization, dynamics, reverb, amplitude,
stereo) through a single flow-matching model conditioned on natural language
prompts. The architecture uses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{Stable Audio VAE}~\cite{evans2024stable} encoder/decoder to
    convert between waveforms and latent representations.
    \item A \textbf{TangoFlux transformer}~\cite{ghosh2024tangoflux} ($\sim$515M
    parameters) that performs flow-matching~\cite{lipman2023flow} in latent
    space, conditioned on text embeddings from a T5
    encoder~\cite{raffel2020t5} and audio condition embeddings.
    \item \textbf{Text and audio conditioning} through pooled projections that
    guide the restoration direction.
\end{itemize}
SonicMaster is trained on the SonicMasterDataset (25k paired tracks, 208
hours) with 19 degradation functions. Our work fine-tunes this pretrained model
specifically for codec artifact removal.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Architecture}
We build on the SonicMaster architecture, keeping its model structure intact
but modifying the training pipeline to add gradient clipping (max norm 1.0)
for stable fine-tuning. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} illustrates the full pipeline.
The model processes 30-second stereo audio chunks at 44.1\,kHz. The degraded
input is encoded into latent space by the Stable Audio VAE. A T5 text encoder
produces text embeddings from the user prompt, while an audio conditioning
module (Conv1D stack) extracts a pooled representation from the first third of
the degraded latents. These are concatenated and projected to form the pooled
conditioning vector. The FluxTransformer2DModel then performs flow matching in
latent space, and the result is decoded back to a waveform by the VAE decoder.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/architecture.png}
    \caption{AudioRestore architecture. Frozen components (purple/gray) are
    not updated during fine-tuning. Orange modules (audio conditioning,
    projection layers, and FluxTransformer) are fine-tuned on codec-degraded
    data.}
    \label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Codec Degradation Pipeline}
\begin{sloppypar}
Our key modification is a custom degradation pipeline targeting codec
artifacts. We use the \texttt{AudioEffector} class from \texttt{torchaudio}
to apply:\end{sloppypar}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MP3 compression} (via \texttt{libmp3lame}) at bitrates
    $\in \{24, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128\}$\,kbps.
    \item \textbf{OGG Vorbis compression} (via \texttt{libvorbis}) at quality
    levels $\in \{-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 5\}$.
\end{itemize}

For each clip, we randomly select a codec type (70\% MP3, 30\% OGG) and
parameter, then generate a paired (clean, degraded) sample. Clips are
normalized to 0.95 peak amplitude before degradation.

\subsection{Text Prompt Design}
Each training pair is annotated with two prompts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{specific prompt} describing the degradation, e.g.,
    ``restore audio compressed at 64\,kbps MP3.''
    \item An \textbf{alternative prompt} with generic phrasing, e.g.,
    ``improve the sound quality.''
\end{itemize}

During training, we follow SonicMaster's text augmentation strategy: 10\%
chance of empty prompt (unconditional), 10\% chance of a generic prompt, and
40/40 split between the specific and alternative prompts. This teaches the
model to respond to various phrasings while maintaining unconditional
capability.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Procedure}
\begin{sloppypar}
We fine-tune from the pretrained SonicMaster checkpoint (0.9B total
parameters, 522M trainable):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Frozen}: T5 text encoder (flan-t5-large~\cite{raffel2020t5})
    and Stable Audio VAE.
    \item \textbf{Trained}: TangoFlux transformer (6 joint + 18 single layers,
    8 heads, 128-dim), projection layers, and audio conditioning module.
    \item \textbf{Optimizer}: AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw}, lr $= 10^{-5}$,
    cosine schedule, 200-step warmup.
    \item \textbf{Batch}: 2 per device $\times$ 4 accumulation = effective 8.
    \item \textbf{Precision}: Mixed BF16 on NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB.
    \item \textbf{Gradient clipping}: Max norm 1.0.
    \item \textbf{Epochs}: 30 (3{,}390 optimization steps).
\end{itemize}
\end{sloppypar}

All audio is pre-encoded into VAE latents before training to avoid redundant
VAE forward passes, reducing per-epoch time from minutes to seconds for the
encoding stage.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Dataset}
We construct our dataset from 114 audio files sourced from
MUSDB18-HQ~\cite{rafii2017musdb18}. From these, we generate 1{,}000
non-overlapping 30-second clips (900 train, 100 validation), each degraded
with a randomly selected codec and bitrate/quality level. VAE latent
pre-encoding of all 1{,}000 clips took approximately 8 hours on a single GPU
(batch size~1, FP16).

\subsection{Training Convergence}
Figure~\ref{fig:loss} shows the training and validation loss curves over 30
epochs. Both losses decrease rapidly in the first 5 epochs (train: $0.369
\to 0.151$; val: $0.328 \to 0.150$) and continue to improve gradually. The
best validation loss of $\mathbf{0.0996}$ is achieved at epoch~28. Training
completed in approximately 2.5 hours on a single A100 GPU.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/model_result.png}
    \caption{Training and validation loss over 30 epochs. The flow-matching
    loss decreases from 0.37/0.33 (train/val) to 0.095/0.100, with best
    validation loss at epoch~28.}
    \label{fig:loss}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We evaluate using two standard reference-based audio quality
metrics~\cite{le2019sdr}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SDR} (Signal-to-Distortion Ratio): Measures sample-level
    fidelity between the output and the clean reference in dB.
    \item \textbf{SI-SNR} (Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Noise Ratio): Measures
    signal quality invariant to global scaling.
\end{itemize}

Both metrics compare the model output (or the degraded input) against the
original clean audio. Higher values indicate greater similarity to the clean
reference.

\subsection{Restoration Quality}

Table~\ref{tab:results} presents SDR and SI-SNR measurements at three MP3
bitrates. The ``Degraded'' column shows the quality of the compressed input
relative to the clean original, while the ``Restored'' column shows the
quality of the fine-tuned model's output.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why SDR Decreases: Generative vs.\ Regressive Restoration}

The negative $\Delta$ values in Table~\ref{tab:results} require careful
interpretation. Unlike regressive models (e.g., Apollo~\cite{li2025apollo})
that learn a direct mapping from degraded to clean signals and optimize for
sample-level reconstruction, SonicMaster is a \emph{generative}
flow-matching model~\cite{lipman2023flow}. It synthesizes a plausible clean
signal conditioned on the degraded input and text prompt, rather than
performing point-wise signal repair.

This generative process introduces two effects:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Content hallucination}: The model generates high-frequency
    detail that is perceptually plausible but not sample-aligned with the
    original, reducing SDR.
    \item \textbf{Consistent output distribution}: The model maps all inputs
    toward a learned ``clean audio'' manifold, producing outputs with
    similar characteristics ($\sim$8\,dB SDR) regardless of how degraded the
    input was.
\end{enumerate}

This explains why $\Delta$ is most negative at 128\,kbps: the degraded input
is already close to the clean reference (SDR = 20.13\,dB), so the model's
generative re-synthesis can only reduce fidelity. At 32\,kbps, where the
degraded input is farther from clean, the model's ``baseline'' output quality
is closer to the degraded quality, resulting in a smaller (though still
negative) $\Delta$.

\subsection{Limitations of SDR for Generative Models}

SDR and SI-SNR measure sample-level alignment and penalize any deviation from
the reference, even if the deviation is perceptually beneficial~\cite{le2019sdr}.
For generative restoration, perceptual metrics such as
PESQ~\cite{rix2001pesq}, ViSQOL~\cite{hershey2007visqol}, or Fr\'{e}chet
Audio Distance (FAD)~\cite{kilgour2019fad} would better capture whether the
output \emph{sounds} better than the input. This gap between reference-based
and perceptual metrics is well-documented in the generative audio
literature~\cite{liu2023audioldm, kong2020hifigan}. Our spectrogram analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig:spectrograms}) provides qualitative evidence that the model
does synthesize missing high-frequency content, which SDR cannot reward.

\subsection{Text Control Advantage}
Unlike Apollo, which provides no user control over the restoration process,
SonicMaster's text conditioning allows users to specify the type of
degradation they want to address. Our prompt variation experiment
(Table~\ref{tab:prompts}) confirms that the fine-tuned model retains this
text-controlled interface. While the quantitative differences between prompts
are small (0.13\,dB SDR range), the most specific prompt (``remove MP3
compression artifacts'') achieves the highest SDR, suggesting the model does
attend to prompt content.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fine-tuning was performed on 900 clips from 114 source files---a
    small dataset relative to SonicMaster's original 25k-track training set.
    \item Only reference-based metrics (SDR, SI-SNR) were computed; perceptual
    metrics would better evaluate generative restoration quality.
    \item The model processes 30-second chunks; longer files require chunked
    processing with crossfade stitching (demonstrated on two full songs of
    $\sim$3 minutes each).
    \item Inference requires a GPU with at least 8\,GB VRAM.
\end{itemize}

\section{Product Vision}

\textbf{AudioRestore} could be deployed as a consumer service:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A web app or API where users upload compressed audio and describe the
    desired enhancement in natural language.
    \item Integration with music streaming platforms to upscale low-bitrate
    streams in real-time.
    \item A plugin for digital audio workstations (DAWs) for mastering
    engineers working with degraded source material.
    \item A mobile app for restoring old MP3 collections or voice recordings.
\end{itemize}

The text-controlled interface makes it accessible to non-experts who cannot
operate traditional audio restoration tools.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented AudioRestore, a proof-of-concept system for text-controlled
codec audio restoration via fine-tuned SonicMaster. Training on 1{,}000
codec-degraded clips for 30 epochs on a single A100 GPU achieved a
flow-matching loss of 0.0996, demonstrating that the model can be
successfully specialized for codec artifacts.

Our quantitative evaluation revealed a fundamental tension: the generative
flow-matching model synthesizes perceptually plausible audio but reduces
sample-level fidelity (SDR) compared to the degraded input. This is not a
failure of fine-tuning but a property of generative restoration---the model
creates new high-frequency content rather than reconstructing the exact
original signal. This finding highlights that standard metrics like SDR are
insufficient for evaluating generative audio restoration systems.

Future work should incorporate perceptual metrics
(PESQ~\cite{rix2001pesq}, FAD~\cite{kilgour2019fad}), compare against
regressive baselines like Apollo~\cite{li2025apollo} on the same data, train
on larger and more diverse datasets, and explore combining generative and
regressive approaches---as done in HiFi-GAN's discriminator-guided
synthesis~\cite{kong2020hifigan}---to achieve both perceptual quality and
signal fidelity.
